The Architecture of Love
The Trinity

Is the Trinity a philosophical corruption of how God is revealed in the Word of God, or is it the mandatory bedrock for understanding God as personal, eternal love?

One of the most common charges leveled against orthodox Christianity by critics, echoed by neighboring monotheistic faiths like Islam and Judaism, and even smaller Oneness christian groups, is that the doctrine of the Trinity is incoherent. How could God be three persons, yet one being?

When we examine the Scriptures both, Old Testament and New, and the philosophical requirements of divine love, a different picture emerges. The Trinity is not a contradiction wrapped in theological jargon; it is the breathtaking answer to how God can be relational in His very essence.

What is the Trinity?

Definition: Within the one Being that is God, there exist eternally three coequal, coeternal Persons, namely, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

God is one Being (monotheism) yet there are three divine, eternal, and equal persons that share this one Being.

It is important to recognize the distiction between the words Being and Person.
Being is what makes something what it is.
Person is what makes someone who he or she is.

In short, Trinitarians believe the God of the Bible is one what but three whos.

Each of the divine Persons shares fully and completely in the divine Being, while at the same time bear a relationship to one another within the Godhead itself.

  • Foundation One: There is only one God (monotheism).
  • Foundation Two: There are three divine persons.
  • Foundation Three: The persons are co-equal and co-eternal.

  • I am the LORD (YHWH), that is My name; And My glory I will not give to another. (Isaiah 42:8)

Common Objections

Deuteronomy 6:4 and the "One God"

Deuteronomy 6:4, famously known as the Shema, declares: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one." Critics often cite this as the ultimate proof against the Trinity, claiming that "one" strictly demands a singular, solitary person.

However, exploring the original languages provides a much broader context. The Hebrew word used for "one" here is echad. While echad can refer to a solitary item, it is very frequently used in Scripture to describe a composite unity. For example, in Genesis 2:24, a husband and wife become "one (echad) flesh." In Ezra 2:64, the entire assembly of Israel is referred to as "one (echad)." In both cases, multiple persons make up a unified whole. (When ancient Hebrew needed to express absolute singularity, it typically used the word yachid).

Similarly, in the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), the Greek word chosen to translate echad in Deuteronomy 6:4 is heis, which also routinely denotes a united one rather than an isolated, solitary figure.

Therefore, the Shema emphatically teaches monotheism, that there is only one divine Being, which is the very first foundational pillar of the Trinity. It does not demand that this one God must be restricted to a single person, perfectly harmonizing with the triune nature of Yahweh revealed throughout the rest of Scripture.

Isaiah 9:6 and the "Everlasting Father"

A common objection raised by Oneness Pentecostals is rooted in Isaiah 9:6, where the prophetic title for Jesus includes the phrase "Everlasting Father". They argue this proves Jesus and the Father are the exact same person.

However, looking closely at the original Hebrew idiom clarifies this title. In Hebrew, the phrase translates more accurately to "Father of Eternity", written ('āḇʿaḏ). In ancient Hebrew culture, calling someone the "father of" (ab) something was a common idiom used to denote that the person was the owner, author, or possessor of that specific thing or quality.

We see this exact naming convention throughout Scripture in other names starting with ab:

  • Abraham: Father of a multitude (possessor of many descendants).
  • Abigale: Father of joy (possessor of joy).
  • Abialbon: Father of strength (possessor of strength).
  • Abiasaph: Father of gathering (possessor of gathering).

By naming Jesus the "Father of Eternity," Isaiah was not confusing the distinct persons of the Trinity. Instead, he was declaring that the Messiah is the very possessor, creator, and owner of eternity itself.
